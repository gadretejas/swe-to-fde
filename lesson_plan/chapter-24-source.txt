<h2 id="section-1">24.1 Customer brief</h2><p>Harbor operates two warehouses, East and West. Coordinators investigate shipment exceptions by comparing event records with notes. The sponsor wants a pilot in East within four weeks and asks for “AI explanations and automatic customer notifications.” Operations wants faster review; security requires warehouse-scoped access; support needs a workflow it can diagnose without the original developer.</p><p>The baseline is a manual evidence checklist. For this simulation, assume 2,000 eligible cases monthly and a candidate six-minute reduction per adopted case. These are planning inputs, not observed results. Validate any benefit claim through the measurement plan you design.</p><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Constraint</th><th>Capstone requirement</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Scope</td><td>Start with an evidence-review workbench for East. No automatic external messages or order changes.</td></tr><tr><td>Identity</td><td>Use explicit synthetic principals and server-side or equivalent trusted policy enforcement in the prototype.</td></tr><tr><td>Data</td><td>Events may be duplicated, delayed, malformed, or arrive out of order.</td></tr><tr><td>Operations</td><td>Show stale or unavailable evidence clearly and preserve a manual fallback.</td></tr><tr><td>Ownership</td><td>Name accountable roles for launch, support, access, and benefit measurement.</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>If you implement only a browser demonstration, label authorization as simulated and explain the trusted enforcement required in a deployed system. Hiding a button is not evidence of access control. Do not send synthetic or real data to an external AI provider as an implicit part of this exercise; a deterministic draft is sufficient for the core slice.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">24.2 Discovery and success contract</h2><p>Draft ten discovery questions, then record explicit assumptions for unanswered ones. Cover the user's decision, source authority, freshness, identity, operational impact, and ownership. Identify the difference between the sponsor's requested feature and the workflow outcome.</p><p>Write acceptance criteria that can be checked. For example: a permitted East coordinator can inspect an East exception; a West principal cannot read that exception through either the main view or an export path; replaying the same source event does not create a second logical event; missing evidence is shown as missing rather than inferred as success.</p><p>Define a quality guardrail as well as a time target. Faster review is not a win if it increases incorrect resolutions. Separate the criteria for a technical prototype, a supervised pilot, and a broader launch.</p><label for="discovery24">Discovery and acceptance contract</label><textarea id="discovery24" data-save="field-24-discovery" rows="12" placeholder="User decision and baseline&#10;Discovery questions and assumptions&#10;In-scope and excluded effects&#10;Functional and failure acceptance criteria&#10;Outcome and quality measures&#10;Owners and decision dates"></textarea>

<h2 id="section-5">24.5 Change request: automatic notifications</h2><p>At the midpoint, the sponsor asks to send customer notifications automatically because the demo looks convincing. <h2 id="section-6">24.6 Launch inject: access defect</h2><p>On the day before the pilot, a test reveals that an East user can export a West record by changing an identifier. The ordinary UI correctly hides West records. A stakeholder argues that the issue is unlikely to occur in normal use.</p><p>Treat this as a failed access boundary. Pause the affected launch path, preserve appropriate evidence, and follow the relevant incident process if exposure actually occurred. Identify the trusted enforcement gap, repair all equivalent paths, and retest allowed and denied cases. Do not use a hidden button or an average test score to excuse the failure.</p><p>Prepare a concise customer update with known impact, current containment, the next investigation step, and the next update time. Avoid speculating about exposure or promising a repair time without evidence. Revisit the launch decision only after the required controls are verified.</p><label for="incident24">Containment and revised launch recommendation</label><textarea id="incident24" data-save="field-24-incident" rows="9" placeholder="Known facts and unknowns&#10;Containment and owner&#10;Repair and equivalent-path review&#10;Retest evidence&#10;Customer update&#10;Conditions for reconsidering launch"></textarea>
<h2 id="section-7">24.7 Rollout, ownership, and outcomes</h2><p>Design a supervised rollout with an explicit cohort, duration, stop conditions, and fallback. Assign roles for support coverage, access changes, operational decisions, and benefit measurement. Show how another engineer can diagnose the prototype from the runbook.</p><p>Google's launch guidance describes coordinated readiness reviews and practical checklists tailored to the launch. Apply the principle by linking each readiness item to evidence and an owner, rather than treating a checked box as proof.</p><p>Use Chapter 22's business-case approach. At 2,000 monthly cases, six minutes saved, and 60% adoption, modeled released capacity is 120 hours monthly. At 30% adoption it is 60 hours. These are hypotheses to validate with representative cases, correction effort, and quality outcomes; they are not cash-saving commitments.</p><p>Prepare a ten-minute briefing: two minutes on the problem and scope, three on design and verification, two on the changes and access defect, and three on rollout, outcomes, and the requested decision. Keep detailed evidence available for questions.</p>
