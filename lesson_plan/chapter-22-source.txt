<p class="eyebrow">MODULE 04 · ADVANCED FIELD ENGINEERING</p><p>Harbor's pilot works. Now its executive sponsor asks whether to fund expansion. A feature tour cannot answer that question. You need to explain the decision, the expected outcome, the alternatives, and the evidence that would justify spending more.</p><div class="lesson-panel"><h3>Learning objectives</h3><ul><li>Translate technical progress into a decision-oriented executive brief.</li><li>Separate measured facts, assumptions, forecasts, and commitments.</li><li>Compare alternatives against an explicit baseline.</li><li>Estimate capacity value without mislabeling it as cash savings.</li><li>Present uncertainty, answer challenges, and record a clear decision.</li></ul><p>Allow 100–130 minutes. Prerequisites: Chapters 11, 15, and 21. Produce a one-page business case and a three-minute briefing. All customer names and numerical examples are fictional.</p></div>
<h2 id="section-1">22.1 Start with the decision</h2><p>Identify who can approve what, by when, and within which constraints. “Update leadership” is a meeting purpose, not a decision. “Approve a six-week supervised expansion within the stated budget, with a review before further rollout” gives the audience something concrete to assess.</p><p>Executive communication should preserve the reasoning that matters while reducing irrelevant implementation detail. A latency improvement matters if it changes the user's workflow or meets an acceptance requirement. If the operational effect is unmeasured, report the technical result honestly and state what remains to be tested.</p><p>Use this sequence: recommendation, customer problem, evidence, options, costs and benefits, uncertainty, and decision requested. Keep detailed architecture and calculations available in an appendix. Do not hide material risks there; risks that could change the decision belong in the main brief.</p><figure><img src="../../assets/images/chapter-22-flow.svg" alt="Evidence informs options, which support a recommendation and an explicit decision."><figcaption>A briefing connects evidence to a decision the audience can make.</figcaption></figure>
<h2 id="section-2">22.2 Separate evidence from inference</h2><p>A pilot observation is not automatically a forecast for every site. Label the sample, period, task definition, and measurement method. Explain whether the comparison used similar cases and whether other changes could account for the result. Include review and correction time in task measurements.</p><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Statement type</th><th>Example</th><th>What to make explicit</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Measured fact</td><td>Observed review time decreased in the pilot sample</td><td>Sample, baseline, measurement method, variation</td></tr><tr><td>Assumption</td><td>60% of eligible cases will use the workflow</td><td>Basis and sensitivity</td></tr><tr><td>Forecast</td><td>Expansion may release 120 hours monthly</td><td>Formula, conditions, uncertainty</td></tr><tr><td>Commitment</td><td>Owner will report adoption after six weeks</td><td>Accountable owner and agreed date</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>Avoid implying causation from a simple before-and-after comparison when training, staffing, case mix, or seasonality changed too. Where a controlled comparison is impractical, state the limitation and gather additional evidence. <h2 id="section-3">22.3 Compare real alternatives</h2><p>Include the current workflow and at least one plausible smaller intervention. For Harbor, the alternatives might be improving the checklist, extending the supervised pilot, or expanding to every warehouse. Compare them against the same objectives and planning horizon.</p><p>Doing nothing new still has consequences: existing operating effort, unresolved defects, and missed opportunities may continue. Conversely, expansion has migration, training, support, and operational costs. Do not construct an obviously poor alternative merely to make the preferred option appear inevitable.</p><div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Option</th><th>Potential advantage</th><th>Key limitation</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Improve the checklist</td><td>Low implementation effort</td><td>May leave repeated manual work</td></tr><tr><td>Supervised expansion</td><td>Tests another site and adoption conditions</td><td>Limited immediate reach; needs review capacity</td></tr><tr><td>Full rollout</td><td>Broader potential impact</td><td>Greater exposure to untested variation and support demand</td></tr></tbody></table></div><p>HM Treasury's Green Book describes appraisal in terms of costs, benefits, and risks of options for achieving objectives. It is UK public-sector guidance; this lesson borrows the comparison discipline, not its jurisdiction-specific approval rules or a claim of formal compliance.</p>
<h2 id="section-4">22.4 Distinguish capacity value from cash</h2><p>If a workflow saves time, staff may handle more cases, reduce backlog, improve quality, or spend time on higher-value tasks. That does not automatically reduce payroll or release budget. A monetary valuation of time is an analytical estimate; a cash-saving claim needs a specific, credible mechanism and an accountable owner.</p><p>Avoid double-counting the same released time as both reduced labor expense and additional productive capacity. Separate potential revenue from confirmed revenue, and do not count all customer revenue as attributable to one feature. Keep difficult-to-price outcomes, such as improved confidence in decisions, visible without inventing precise dollar values.</p><p>Include setup, integration, training, adoption assistance, recurring service charges, support, and measurement effort. Use a consistent currency and horizon. For a real approval, align costing and benefit categories with the organization's finance owner. The calculator below is a simplified educational model, not a discounted cash-flow analysis.</p>

The pilot suggests a workflow improvement, but performance at another warehouse is untested.</p><p><strong>Options:</strong> Improve the checklist, expand under supervision, or roll out broadly. Recommend supervised expansion to test site variation and adoption before increasing exposure.</p><p><strong>Value scenario:</strong> Under the lab's assumptions, six-month modeled net capacity value is 6,000; at half the adoption rate it is −12,000. Neither number is a cash-saving commitment.</p><p><strong>Conditions:</strong> Named operations owner, approved access boundaries, measured review time and quality, and a fallback to the existing workflow.</p><p><strong>Next decision:</strong> Review actual adoption, outcomes, costs, and support burden after six weeks before authorizing further scope.</p></div><p>Replace every illustrative assertion with actual evidence before using this brief externally. <h2 id="section-7">22.7 Deliver the three-minute briefing</h2><p>Use the opening to state the recommendation and requested decision. Spend the middle on the evidence and alternatives. Close with the material uncertainty, next validation step, and action needed from the audience. Practice aloud; a page that reads well may still be too dense to present.</p><p>Use charts only when they clarify a comparison. Label units, time horizons, and whether values are measured or modeled. Keep the baseline and scenario assumptions visible. A chart showing cumulative benefit without cumulative cost can create a misleading impression even if every plotted number is correct.</p><p>Prepare short answers to likely challenges: “Why this option?”, “What if adoption is lower?”, “Who owns the outcome?”, and “What would make us stop?” If a question requires evidence you do not have, say what is known, what is unknown, and when you will return with an answer.</p>
<h2 id="section-8">22.8 Handle challenges without overstating certainty</h2><section class="lesson-panel"><h3>Executive challenge</h3><p>The sponsor says, “So we can remove 120 paid hours from next month's budget?” What is the strongest answer?</p><label><input type="radio" name="decision22" value="0" data-save="practice-22-decision"> Yes; every hour saved automatically becomes cash savings.</label><br><label><input type="radio" name="decision22" value="1" data-save="practice-22-decision"> The model estimates released capacity; a budget reduction needs a separate validated mechanism and owner.</label><br><label><input type="radio" name="decision22" value="2" data-save="practice-22-decision"> Avoid the question and show the architecture diagram.</label><p><button id="decision-check22" type="button">Review my response</button></p><p id="decision-result22" role="status"></p></section><p>Disagreement can reveal different objectives. Operations may value backlog reduction while finance needs budget certainty. Record both rather than forcing them into one unsupported return figure. If executives choose a different option, document the decision, assumptions, owner, and follow-up evidence.</p><p>After the meeting, confirm the agreed scope and conditions. Approval to investigate is not approval to deploy. Approval for one warehouse is not approval for all warehouses. Update the delivery plan so the executive decision and the engineering commitments remain aligned.</p>
<h2 id="section-9">22.9 Business-case workshop</h2><p><strong>Scenario:</strong> Harbor's sponsor wants full rollout based on the default lab model. The time-saving estimate comes from 30 relatively simple cases at one site. Adoption is forecast at 60%, but the second site's workflow owner is not yet committed. Support effort is included in recurring cost as an estimate, not a measured figure. Finance asks for a guaranteed six-month cash saving.</p><p>Spend 25–35 minutes drafting a one-page recommendation and a three-minute talk track. Compare the baseline and a smaller experiment. Calculate the 60% and 30% adoption scenarios, explain the cash-versus-capacity distinction, and propose evidence that could change the decision. Include a named role for each benefit and risk, without inventing actual customer personnel.</p><label for="workshop22">Workshop recommendation and talk track</label><textarea id="workshop22" data-save="field-22-workshop" rows="12" placeholder="Recommendation and requested decision&#10;Evidence limits and alternatives&#10;Base and downside scenarios&#10;Capacity value versus cash&#10;Validation, owners, and stop conditions&#10;Three-minute briefing outline"></textarea><details class="lesson-panel"><summary>Worked response outline</summary><p>Recommend a supervised second-site trial rather than guaranteeing broad rollout benefits.
