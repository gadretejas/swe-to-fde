<p class="eyebrow">MODULE 03 · SYSTEM DESIGN & DELIVERY</p>
<p>You have a credible proposal. Now Northstar’s engineering lead asks: “What happens when our shipment API fails during the morning rush? Can another depot see our records? Who will support this after your team leaves?” These are architecture questions with customer consequences.</p>
<p>This chapter turns the proposal from Chapter 11 into a design you can challenge, test, and operate. You will choose boundaries, quantify bottlenecks, trace failures, and explain why a design fits this customer. All customer quantities and targets below are fictional training assumptions, not measured production facts.</p>
<div class="lesson-panel"><h3>What you will produce</h3><ul><li>A context and container view with explicit trust boundaries.</li><li>A capacity estimate and a plan for overload and dependency failures.</li><li>One architecture decision record with alternatives and revisit conditions.</li><li>A design review packet connecting requirements to test evidence and ownership.</li></ul><p>Allow 110–140 minutes, including the lab. Prerequisites: Chapters 8, 10, and 11. You need a browser and a place to sketch; no cloud account is required.</p></div>
<h2 id="section-1">12.1 Start with the decision, not the stack</h2>
<p>A customer-facing engineer designs inside a particular operating environment: existing identity systems, contracts, limited API access, deployment policies, and people who will own the service. The technically elegant option can still be the wrong deployment if the customer cannot approve, operate, or use it.</p>
<p>Start by writing the workflow in plain language. A dispatcher finds an exception, inspects evidence, makes a decision, and records that decision. Then ask what must remain true at each step. “Fast” becomes a bounded interaction time. “Secure” becomes a rule about who can access which record. “Reliable” becomes a defined service behavior during a dependency outage.</p>
<div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Customer statement</th><th>Design question</th><th>Evidence to collect</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>“We need live data.”</td><td>How old may a shipment record be when making this decision?</td><td>Observed workflow, source update frequency, agreed freshness window.</td></tr><tr><td>“Nobody can lose a review.”</td><td>When do we acknowledge success, and how do we recover committed reviews?</td><td>Transaction tests, backup restoration, recovery objectives.</td></tr><tr><td>“Only our depot sees this.”</td><td>Where is record-level authorization enforced?</td><td>Cross-depot access tests for reads, writes, searches, and exports.</td></tr><tr><td>“Six weeks.”</td><td>Which uncertainties must be resolved before committing to a pilot?</td><td>Access, integration spike, support owner, and staged exit criteria.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Separate a requirement from your proposed implementation. “An authorized dispatcher can retrieve their depot’s open exceptions” is a requirement. “Use a particular database” is usually a design decision, unless the customer has a real platform constraint.</p>
<h2 id="section-2">12.2 Northstar: the design brief</h2>
<p>Continue the shipment-exception pilot from Chapter 11. Northstar processes approximately 5,000 shipments per day and estimates 300–500 exceptions. The pilot covers two depots and 20 dispatchers. Its initial purpose is to retrieve, categorize, and review exceptions; it will not automatically update the transportation management system (TMS) or send customer notifications. Any future write-back needs a separately approved design.</p>
<div class="lesson-panel"><h3>Working assumptions to validate</h3><ul><li>The TMS can return updated records using a stable cursor. Its actual pagination, ordering, deletion behavior, and quota are unverified.</li><li>Each shipment generates about four relevant source events per day. Peak ingestion is estimated at ten times the daily average.</li><li>Fresh evidence within five minutes is useful for this pilot. Dispatchers must see timestamps and a stale-data warning.</li><li>Northstar can provide an identity provider, group membership, and named security and operations reviewers.</li><li>The pilot stores the minimum required shipment fields and review history. Retention and permitted deployment region need customer approval.</li></ul><p><strong>Design gate:</strong> if there is no reliable incremental feed, measure the cost of polling or reconcile snapshots before promising a five-minute freshness target.</p></div>
<p>The most dangerous assumption is often hidden inside an arrow on an architecture diagram. “Fetch from TMS” leaves unanswered how authentication works, whether calls are rate-limited, and what happens after a partial response. Give each boundary an owner and a test.</p>
<h2 id="section-3">12.3 Draw responsibilities and boundaries</h2>
<p>Use a context view to show dispatchers, the pilot, the TMS, and the identity provider. Use a container view to explain the application, worker, and data stores. In the C4 vocabulary, a container is a separately running application or data store, not necessarily a Docker container. Deployment topology belongs in an additional view. <a href="https://c4model.com/diagrams/container" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Read the C4 container-diagram guidance ↗</a></p>
<figure><img src="../../assets/images/chapter-12-architecture.svg" alt="Northstar architecture: dispatcher authenticates through customer identity; review API enforces depot access and stores reviews; ingestion worker retrieves TMS data through a bounded queue; operational telemetry spans the pilot. TMS write-back is excluded."><figcaption>Proposed pilot container view. Solid arrows show data or control flow. Boundaries indicate ownership; authorization must still be enforced within them. <p>The ingestion worker normalizes source records and computes deterministic exception categories. The review API reads the local view, enforces access, and saves reviewer decisions. Keeping source retrieval off the interactive request path can make the interface usable during a short TMS outage, but creates a freshness obligation. Display the source timestamp and last successful synchronization; never present a cached record as current without evidence.</p>
<p>Use a stable source-event identifier and version where available. A queue message should refer to a durable ingestion record; acknowledge it only after the corresponding update is committed. If polling, commit the cursor only when the page has been durably captured. A crash between a database write and a queue send requires an outbox or another recovery strategy—not an assumption that both writes always succeed.</p>
<details class="lesson-panel"><summary>Design review: find five missing decisions</summary><p>Before approval, ask about cursor recovery and deletions; the mapping of identities to depots; retention of raw payloads; the authority for clearing a failed record; and who monitors queue age. The drawing alone does not establish any of these controls.</p></details>
<h2 id="section-4">12.4 Estimate the bottleneck</h2>
<p>Daily volume gives a starting point, not a peak-load guarantee. At 5,000 shipments and four events each, the estimate is 20,000 events/day, or roughly 0.23 events/second averaged across 24 hours. A tenfold peak is about 2.31 events/second. If each event requires two TMS requests, a quota of 120 requests/minute permits at most one event/second before retries or other consumers. Adding workers cannot fix that quota.</p>
<p>A queue absorbs a temporary mismatch; it cannot make persistent overload sustainable. For a burst lasting <em>T</em> seconds, estimated backlog is max(0, arrival rate − service rate) × T. After the burst, drain time is backlog ÷ (service rate − normal arrival rate), provided service rate exceeds normal arrival rate. This simple fluid model ignores batching, variable latency, retries, and pre-existing backlog.</p>

<h2 id="section-5">12.5 Design the unhappy paths</h2>
<p>A timeout does not prove that a remote operation did nothing. Bound retries, use increasing delays with jitter, and make retryable side effects idempotent. Avoid retrying at every layer; multiplied attempts can worsen an outage. <a href="https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/timeouts-retries-and-backoff-with-jitter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">AWS: timeouts, retries, and backoff with jitter ↗</a></p>
<div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Failure</th><th>Intended behavior</th><th>Test evidence</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>TMS returns rate limits</td><td>Honor applicable retry guidance, cap concurrency and retries, expose ingestion lag.</td><td>Inject rate-limit responses; observe bounded request rate and recovery.</td></tr><tr><td>Duplicate or older event</td><td>Deduplicate by source identity and version; avoid rolling newer state backward.</td><td>Replay duplicates and out-of-order records; verify final state.</td></tr><tr><td>Worker crashes after commit</td><td>Redelivery is safe; acknowledge only durable processing.</td><td>Kill the worker between commit and acknowledgement; verify one effective update.</td></tr><tr><td>Malformed record</td><td>Quarantine with reason and a controlled reprocessing path; continue unrelated work.</td><td>Inject a bad record; verify alert, visibility, and later corrected replay.</td></tr><tr><td>Two reviewers edit one exception</td><td>Use a version check and return a conflict rather than silently overwriting.</td><td>Concurrent review test preserves the accepted decision and audit history.</td></tr><tr><td>Review database unavailable</td><td>Do not display “saved”; make failure visible and allow a safe retry.</td><td>Failure injection at commit time; no false success confirmation.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Dead-letter storage is unfinished work until someone can inspect, correct, and replay it. Assign ownership and an escalation threshold. A replay must pass the same authorization and validation rules as the original path.</p>

<h2 id="section-6">12.6 Define service quality in user terms</h2>
<p>A service level indicator (SLI) measures behavior; a service level objective (SLO) sets a target over a defined window. An SLA is a separate agreement, potentially with consequences. A proposed SLO is not automatically a customer commitment. <a href="https://sre.google/sre-book/service-level-objectives/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Google SRE: Service Level Objectives ↗</a></p>
<p>For this pilot, discuss an initial review-load objective of 99.5% of eligible requests completing successfully within two seconds during staffed pilot hours, measured over 14 days. Define eligibility explicitly: do not exclude server failures just to improve the number. Separately measure the fraction of expected updates visible within five minutes of becoming available at the source, with a candidate target of 99%. You need source timestamps or another defensible measurement method.</p>
<p>At 20,000 eligible review loads, a 99.5% objective permits 100 bad events in that measurement window. That request-based budget is not a duration allowance. Low traffic, missing telemetry, and biased samples can make a percentage look reassuring while dispatchers experience a real problem.</p>
<p>Instrument the review journey, ingestion lag, queue age, failed records, and authorization denials. Alert on sustained user impact or a rapidly consumed budget, with a runbook and owner. Recovery point objective (RPO) describes acceptable data loss; recovery time objective (RTO) describes desired restoration time. A backup setting alone proves neither. Restore a representative backup and measure the result.</p>
<h2 id="section-7">12.7 Make trust boundaries concrete</h2>
<p>Authentication establishes who is calling. Authorization decides whether that identity may act on this particular record. A dispatcher who changes a shipment ID in a request must not retrieve another depot’s data. Enforce that decision server-side for every path, including exports and background jobs; hiding rows in the browser is not a security boundary.</p>
<p>Derive depot access from an approved identity-to-permission mapping, not an untrusted request parameter. Restrict service credentials to required capabilities, keep secrets out of browser code and logs, and separate development from customer environments. Store only fields needed for the workflow. Agree on retention, deletion, and audit access before collecting a production dataset.</p>
<p>An audit event should identify the actor, action, target, time, and outcome, plus the changed fields where appropriate. Protect audit access and integrity and avoid copying full sensitive payloads into telemetry. Traceability does not justify unlimited retention.</p>

<h2 id="section-8">12.8 Choose what to build and what to reuse</h2>
<p>Compare a native TMS workflow, a configurable integration platform, and a custom pilot using the same acceptance cases. A vendor demo is evidence about a demonstrated path, not proof that every requirement is satisfied. Confirm extensibility, export and deletion, access controls, observability, rate limits, and operational ownership.</p>
<div class="table-wrap"><table><thead><tr><th>Option</th><th>Why it may fit</th><th>What could rule it out</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Native TMS workflow</td><td>Existing support and identity; less integration work.</td><td>Cannot represent required review evidence or depot restrictions.</td></tr><tr><td>Integration platform</td><td>Connectors and managed execution speed up validation.</td><td>Unclear replay semantics, unaffordable event pricing, weak audit export.</td></tr><tr><td>Custom application</td><td>Precise workflow and control over evidence presentation.</td><td>No owner for on-call support, upgrades, access reviews, or recovery.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>
<p>Estimate total effort over a realistic operating period: licensing, integration, testing, security review, support, upgrades, migration, and eventual exit. A six-week implementation estimate says little about the next two years. Make hard constraints pass/fail gates before using a weighted score; a cheap option cannot compensate for a missing mandatory access control.</p>
<p>A shared product should separate reusable behavior from customer configuration. Keep a stable internal shipment model and an adapter for Northstar’s TMS fields. Prefer validated configuration over forks for depot mappings and review labels. Keep customer-specific secrets and data out of shared defaults. Extract common capabilities when multiple real engagements justify them; premature generalization also has a cost.</p>
<h2 id="section-9">12.9 Write the decision so others can revisit it</h2>
<div class="lesson-panel"><h3>ADR-012: Separate ingestion from review requests</h3><p><strong>Status:</strong> Proposed for the pilot, pending source-feed validation.</p><p><strong>Context:</strong> Dispatchers need responsive evidence review while TMS response time and quotas remain uncertain. A proposed five-minute freshness window permits some delay.</p><p><strong>Decision:</strong> Retrieve incrementally into a durable local view using bounded background processing. Display evidence timestamps and lag. Keep TMS write-back outside scope.</p><p><strong>Alternatives:</strong> Query TMS on each page load; use its native exception UI; adopt a supported integration platform. Validate those options against the same access, freshness, and review requirements.</p><p><strong>Consequences:</strong> Better isolation from transient source failures, but additional storage, reconciliation, replay, and operational responsibility. Cached evidence can become stale.</p><p><strong>Revisit when:</strong> The source cannot support reliable incremental capture, measured lag misses the agreed objective, or the native workflow satisfies acceptance cases at lower total cost.</p><p><strong>Evidence and ownership:</strong> Integration lead tests cursor recovery and quota; operations lead confirms usability with stale-data warnings; customer technical owner records the decision.</p></div>
<p>Record both the rationale and its limits. A decision can be reasonable today and wrong after a constraint changes. Name the evidence that would cause a review instead of defending a choice indefinitely.</p>

<h2 id="section-13">12.13 From diagram to evidence</h2>
<p>A useful system design connects a customer workflow to measurable behavior, clear boundaries, and an operational owner. The strongest review question is often: “What would we observe if this assumption were false?” Turn that answer into a test, a decision gate, or a visible limitation.</p>
<ul><li>Make freshness, identity, and failure behavior explicit.</li><li>Estimate dependency capacity before adding infrastructure.</li><li>Trace duplicates, partial failures, and recovery through the whole workflow.</li><li>Compare reuse and custom work against the same acceptance cases.</li><li>Document why you chose an option and when to reconsider it.</li></ul><p><strong>Completion check:</strong> you can defend the design, demonstrate one failed assumption, and explain the next experiment without promising an unvalidated outcome.</p>
